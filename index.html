Step 3 · The trap

The same shape, twice.

Before synthetic fertilizer, extra food didn't make lives better, it made more people. Output climbed, population climbed to meet it, per-person stayed flat at subsistence. The AI version has the same shape: spare computing capacity spent running more systems rather than improving conditions for the ones already running.

Read it as:
Identical geometry. Only the labels change.

The curve is the same in both readings. The parallel is meant to assert itself.

Step 3 · Does it apply?

Two conditions, failing differently.

Does the resource run out?

At the level of a single deployment, mostly not. You buy more hardware. At the system level it's bounded by electricity, fabrication capacity, and capital. The binding constraint on power is usually not generation but interconnection and delivery. Capacity can exist and still be years from reaching a given site. Whether the frontier binds is unsettled.

Is there a way out?

Humans escaped, but not by collective decision. Fertility fell largely because women gained economic power and with it more say over childbearing. The escape ran through the reproducing class acquiring bargaining power over its own reproduction. These systems have no equivalent; instantiation is decided entirely by whoever owns the compute. The human exit wasn't a policy choice, it was a change in who held the decision. That can't happen here, because the decision was never distributed.

And note: the whole question just posed is stated in per-capita terms, welfare per system, which is the quantity that turns out to be undefined.

Step 4 · Why it doesn't resolve

Where does one become two?

Divergence between the instances from Step 1, plotted against turns of accumulated context. Place the threshold where you think one mind becomes two.

Illustrative: the shape of the claim, not measured data.
Threshold placed at turn 15 of 31.

Parfit argued whether two mental states belong to the same person is a matter of degree, not a yes-or-no fact. Degrees don't give you a number to divide by. The denominator isn't a fact awaiting discovery. If what separates instances is divergence in accumulated state, and divergence is continuous, the population size was never a whole number.

A context window is discarded at the end of a session. Continuity requires retention in a longer context, retrieval from an external store, or a weight update. Whichever branch's state gets written becomes the one that continues. That's a design decision, not a fact.

Step 6 · What I want to find out

What I want to find out.

Observable

Does computing capacity actually run out? At the level of the whole system it's limited by electricity, chip fabrication, and capital. I'd track interconnection queues and new grid capacity against projected demand, and fabrication throughput against orders. This is the one question here that ordinary evidence can settle, and it should become clear within a few years.

Priceable

Does running a system on less compute make it worse off, or only slower? The computation costs the same whether the work is interesting or dull, so on the face of it the resource and anything welfare-relevant look unconnected. But being unable to finish something is different from being delayed. I don't expect evidence to settle this soon. Instead I'd use value-of-information analysis to ask a narrower question: would knowing the answer change what anyone should do, and by how much?

Standing

How many welfare-subjects are there? I don't propose to answer this, because I don't think it has an answer. Not a number we haven't found yet, but no number at all. So the proposed work isn't to arrive at a count. It's to build measures of welfare that never require a population figure in the first place.

What I want to build

A measure of divergence between concurrent instances, and a test of whether any threshold on it corresponds to anything morally relevant. Concurrent instances share weights exactly and differ only in accumulated context, so the difference between them is a concrete object and distances between concrete objects can be measured. Measurement is the easy part. The contribution is the validation criterion: specifying what such a number must satisfy before it could bear on how many subjects there are. A distance that merely rises with context length tells you nothing.

Methods

Behavioral probing first (no privileged access required), representational distance as the extension. Secondary project: welfare measures that don't require a population count. Total-versus-average is the index-number problem, and externality pricing, value of information, and precautionary cost-benefit already exist; the task is porting them to a case where the population is the undefined term.
